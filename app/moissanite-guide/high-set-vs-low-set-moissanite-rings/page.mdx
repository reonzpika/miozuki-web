# High vs Low Moissanite Ring Settings

<GuideSchema title="High vs Low Moissanite Ring Settings" path="/moissanite-guide/high-set-vs-low-set-moissanite-rings" updated="2026-11-05" />

A high-set moissanite ring holds its centre stone further above the finger, while a low-set ring keeps it closer to the hand. That difference changes how the ring looks from the side and how much it sticks up. A lower setting may suit you if you prefer a ring that sits close to your hand. A higher setting may appeal if you like seeing more of the centre stone from the side.

The useful question is how that profile will fit into your day. Before choosing from the [moissanite ring collection](/collections/moissanite-rings), look beyond the photograph taken from above. A ring can have the shape and sparkle you enjoy while feeling quite different when you see it from the side.

## High and low settings at a glance

| What you are comparing | Higher setting | Lower setting |
|---|---|---|
| Side appearance | A centre stone standing further above the finger | A centre stone sitting closer to the finger |
| Day-to-day consideration | More projection to account for around sleeves, pockets and nearby objects | Less projection, although claws and other details can still catch |
| What may appeal | A raised centre that stands out from the side | A ring that sits close to your hand |
| What to check | The finished height, supporting structure and how it feels on the hand | The finished height, claw shape and whether anything presses against the finger |

Neither column describes every ring of that type. Use the comparison to narrow what you want, then look at the actual design.

## What are you looking at from the side?

The centre stone needs a setting to hold it. That setting may lift it above the band or nestle it closer to the finger. Around it, the shoulders of the band may rise towards the stone, and a basket or supporting rail may sit beneath it.

[GIA's guide to ring settings](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/) explains these parts and the different ways a gemstone can be held. For your choice, the most useful thing is to notice the whole outline, from the band to the highest part of the ring.

Try looking at a side photograph in two passes. First, follow the centre stone: how far does its top rise above the band? Then look beneath and beside it. Is the structure open, enclosed, narrow or spread out? Those details help explain why two rings with similar-looking centres may have different profiles.

You do not need to learn every setting name before asking about a ring. “I like the view from above, but I would prefer it to sit closer to my hand” is a clear starting point. It tells the seller what matters without assuming that the design can be altered.

## When a lower setting may suit you

A lower setting is worth exploring if you want your ring to sit nearer your hand. You may prefer that outline aesthetically, or you may be thinking about the small movements that fill your day: reaching into a pocket, pulling on a sleeve or putting your hand into a bag.

Less projection can be useful in those situations, but height is only part of the picture. The edges and finish of the claws matter too. A low ring with an exposed or catching detail should not be treated as automatically easier to wear than every higher design.

GIA notes that [higher-profile diamond rings can be more likely to catch on things](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/how-to-select-round-diamond-engagement-ring/). That is a useful setting-design consideration when looking at a moissanite ring as well, rather than a prediction that a particular piece will snag. The actual construction still needs attention.

If sleeves or gloves are your concern, say so when enquiring. Ask to see the ring's side and the way the claws finish over the stone. If you can try a sample, notice whether you can make your usual gentle hand movements comfortably. There is no need to deliberately rub a ring against fabric to test it.

For work that has rules about jewellery, follow those rules. Choosing a lower profile does not, by itself, establish that a ring is suitable for the workplace or can be worn under required gloves.

I’d favour a lower profile if the raised centre is the one feature you already know you do not enjoy. That gives you a useful starting point for your shortlist.

## When a higher setting may suit you

A raised setting can make the centre feel like a more distinct feature of the ring, particularly in profile. You may enjoy seeing the stone above the band, the space around it or the supporting metalwork beneath it. Those are good reasons to explore a higher design.

If you like how a raised centre looks from the side, ask for a photo or short video that shows that angle clearly. If you have only seen the ring from above, check the side view before deciding how high you want it to sit.

More height also means more of the setting projects above the finger. Picture the activities in which that might matter to you. Someone happy to remove a ring for particular activities may make a different choice from someone who wants the least noticeable profile during ordinary wear.

A higher ring is not automatically an impractical choice. Equally, liking the look does not mean you should ignore the feel. If you try one, pay attention to whether the height feels comfortable and whether you find yourself adjusting how you move your hand around it.

I’d keep a raised setting on the shortlist if you genuinely enjoy that view. Then compare it with a lower design you also like. The comparison is more useful when you are choosing between two appealing rings, rather than asking one style to win every practical and visual category.

## Height is different from solitaire, pavé or halo style

Solitaire, pavé and halo describe other features of a ring. A solitaire centres the design on one main stone; pavé describes closely set smaller stones; a halo surrounds the centre with a frame of accents. None of those names gives you the finished height of the ring.

A plain band does not prove the centre sits low. A halo does not prove it sits high. Look at the side rather than assigning height from the amount of decoration seen from above. Our [solitaire, pavé and halo moissanite ring comparison](/moissanite-guide/solitaire-vs-pave-vs-halo-moissanite-rings) covers those visual choices separately.

Cathedral is another name you may encounter. It describes shoulders that rise to support the centre setting, as [GIA illustrates in its setting guide](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/). The term helps you picture the structure, but a side view and a stated measurement are more useful when comparing the height of two actual rings.

## Does a higher setting mean more sparkle?

Setting height is not a sparkle grade. It is a description of how the stone and its setting sit above the hand. A height label alone does not establish that one moissanite ring will look brighter than another.

If sparkle is deciding the choice, ask to see the actual rings in similar lighting and from similar angles. Comparing one close-up under bright spotlights with another photograph in soft light will not isolate the effect of setting height.

You may prefer the way a raised centre is presented, or enjoy how much of the stone's side you can see. Choose the appearance you enjoy, then check that the profile feels right for you too.

## Choose around the part of your day that matters most

| Your priority | Where to begin | What to confirm before choosing |
|---|---|---|
| A ring that sits close to your hand | A lower-profile design you like from above | Side view, finished height and how the underside feels |
| An elevated centre you enjoy seeing in profile | A higher design with the side detail you want | How its projection feels during ordinary hand movements |
| Less to work around near sleeves or pockets | Compare lower profiles first | Claw shape and finishing, not height alone |
| A particular wedding-band pairing | Your preferred engagement ring, viewed from the side | Actual clearance and contact beside the proposed band |
| Shopping online without trying the ring | A design with clear photographs from several angles | Missing height information and the exact option being shown |

If you plan to wear a wedding band beside it, use our [wedding-band fit guide](/moissanite-guide/wedding-band-fit-engagement-ring) for the clearance and contact checks. A high centre does not automatically make a straight band fit beneath it, and a low centre does not rule out every pairing.

Once you have narrowed the profile, compare the other details that affect the finished ring: the metal, finger size and centre-stone choice. The [moissanite ring choosing guide](/moissanite-guide/how-to-choose-a-moissanite-ring) brings those decisions together. If you are comparing silver options, the [sterling silver and plating guide](/moissanite-guide/sterling-silver-moissanite-rings) explains what the material descriptions mean.

For wearing, cleaning and storage advice, follow the [jewellery care guide](/pages/jewellery-care-guide) and any instructions for the particular piece. A lower setting does not make a ring suitable for every activity.

## What to ask before ordering online

You can make an enquiry quite simple:

> “I like this moissanite ring, but I prefer a setting that sits close to my hand. Could you show me the side profile, confirm its finished height and explain where that measurement is taken?”

Add the design name, the material and centre size you are considering. If a particular routine matters, mention it. Ask whether the photograph shows that exact configuration, especially where a listing offers more than one option. These details make it easier to have a useful conversation without assuming every version has identical dimensions.

You can [ask Miozuki about a shortlisted ring](/pages/bespoke-order) before ordering. If you would prefer a different height, ask which configurations are available for that design before deciding.

The right profile is the one whose appearance you enjoy and whose practical trade-offs you understand. Start with the view that draws you in, then give the side of the ring the same attention as the top.
