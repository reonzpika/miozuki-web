# Oval vs Elongated Cushion Moissanite Rings: Shape, Size and Sparkle

<GuideSchema title="Oval vs Elongated Cushion Moissanite Rings: Shape, Size and Sparkle" path="/moissanite-guide/oval-vs-elongated-cushion-moissanite-rings" updated="2026-11-10" />

An oval moissanite has a curved outline with rounded ends. An elongated cushion is longer than it is wide, with rounded corners that give it a more rectangular shape. If you are choosing between them, look first at the outline you enjoy, then compare the actual measurements and how each stone looks in motion.

The two rings below show how these shapes look in Miozuki designs. Look at the centre first, then decide how much detail you enjoy around it.

## The difference at a glance

| What you are comparing | Oval moissanite | Elongated cushion moissanite |
|---|---|---|
| Centre-stone outline | Curved sides flowing into rounded ends | A rectangular outline softened by rounded corners |
| First detail to notice | The continuous curve around the stone | The corners and how full or narrow the outline looks |
| A useful starting point | You prefer a flowing, rounded outline | You enjoy rounded corners and a more rectangular outline |

For either shape, compare the actual length and width for size, and watch the individual stone in moving video to judge its sparkle.

## Oval or elongated cushion: what changes in the shape?

An oval's curve continues around the stone without forming distinct corners. An elongated cushion has corners, although they are rounded rather than pointed. Its sides may look fuller or straighter depending on the individual cut.

The word elongated simply means the cushion is longer than it is wide. It does not tell you how narrow it will be. One elongated cushion can look quite broad, while another looks much longer. Ovals vary in their proportions too.

I would look at the centre stone's edge before comparing the sparkle. If the outline already feels right to you, you have a useful starting point. You can then decide how much detail you want around it.

## Which looks bigger on the hand?

There is no single answer based on the shape name or carat label alone. Look at the centre stone's length and width in millimetres, then consider the space taken up by the setting.

Length tells you how far the centre extends along the finger. Width helps you picture how much space it takes across the finger. A longer stone can still be quite narrow, so checking only the length can give you the wrong impression.

A halo also adds an outline around the centre stone. If one ring has a halo and the other does not, the whole ring can look more substantial for reasons beyond the stone's shape.

## Which shape has more sparkle?

Shape alone cannot tell you which individual stone you will find more lively. The cut, the arrangement of its flat surfaces, called facets, and the lighting all influence what you see.

It helps to separate two effects. White flashes make a stone look bright as it moves. Coloured flashes are often called fire. A still photograph can capture one bright moment while missing the way those flashes change.

My advice is to ask for a short video of each stone moving slowly in similar lighting. Watch the centre as well as the edges. Notice whether you enjoy the size and pattern of the flashes, and whether a dark area changes as the stone turns or stays prominent through much of the movement.

You may see descriptions such as brilliant, crushed ice or antique-style. Ask what those words describe on the particular stone. An elongated outline does not specify one facet arrangement, and a product name alone cannot show you how it performs in light.

GIA's guidance on cut gemstones discusses outline, facet arrangement and finish as different parts of judging the cut. Those are useful things to look at, rather than treating the shape name as a sparkle rating. ## How much does the setting change the choice?

Quite a lot can change around the same centre shape. A halo creates another border of detail. A pavé band adds small stones along the finger. A plain band leaves more visible metal around the centre.

Once you know which centre shape you prefer, look at the decoration you would enjoy wearing with it.

## Which shape would suit your style?

An oval is a useful place to begin if you enjoy a continuous curved outline. Look at how narrow or full you want it to be, then decide whether you like a plain or decorated band.

An elongated cushion may appeal if rounded corners are the detail that catches your eye. Compare a few actual outlines before deciding, since the word cushion covers more than one set of proportions.

If you are drawn to a fuller, more decorated ring, pay attention to the halo and band too. You may find that the setting explains your preference as much as the centre stone does.

I would bring it back to the view you will see most often. Which ring do you enjoy looking down at on your hand? A shape does not need to follow a rule about finger length to be a good choice for you.
