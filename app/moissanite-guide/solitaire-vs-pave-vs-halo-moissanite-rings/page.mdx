A plain-band solitaire draws your eye to the centre stone. A pavé band carries smaller stones along the finger, while a halo frames the centre with another outline of stones. The useful question is which arrangement you enjoy looking at as a whole.

To make that choice easier, this guide compares three actual Miozuki designs: the Classic Solitaire, the Pavé Solitaire and the Round Halo Pavé. Each listing gives a round centre-stone size of 6.5 mm, so you can compare the surrounding design without immediately moving to a different centre size. If you are choosing a moissanite ring in NZ, these are practical examples to explore alongside the wider [moissanite ring collection](/collections/moissanite-rings).

## One useful distinction before comparing

The names can overlap. Pavé describes closely set small stones; a halo describes a frame around a centre stone. That means a halo ring can also have a pavé band, as the Miozuki example does. “Pavé Solitaire” likewise keeps a prominent centre while adding shoulder detail.

[GIA’s guide to ring settings](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/) explains this distinction between the way stones are set and the overall design. Here, the three product names give us a simpler way to discuss the actual choices. For the broader introduction to shapes, styles and metals, see our [guide to choosing a moissanite ring](/moissanite-guide/how-to-choose-a-moissanite-ring).

## Classic Solitaire: let the centre lead

In the Classic Solitaire photograph, there is a clear change between the polished band and the centre stone. With no row of shoulder stones or surrounding halo, the round outline is easy to follow. The metal remains a visible part of the design rather than becoming a background for more gems.

This is where I’d begin if your favourite detail is the centre itself. Start with the round shape and plain band, then compare the pavé version to see whether you miss the shoulder detail.

Look at the shoulders as well as the stone. The polished surfaces and the way the band meets the setting contribute to the whole ring. A plain band does not mean there is nothing else to notice; the balance between metal and stone is simply more exposed.

It may also help to imagine the ring alongside the jewellery you already wear. Do you enjoy the polished metal, or would you prefer a line of smaller stones along the band? That is the main visual choice between the Classic and the Pavé Solitaire.

## Pavé Solitaire: carry the detail along the band

The Pavé Solitaire photograph shows a different route for your eye. The centre still has its own outline, while the small shoulder stones extend the decoration to either side. Rather than adding a frame around the centre, this design adds detail along the band.

I’d put this beside the Classic if you keep returning to the centre shape but want something more detailed around it. Ask yourself which part of the pavé ring appeals to you: the line of small stones, the way the shoulders meet the centre, or the combination. That makes the comparison more useful than simply asking which one has “more sparkle”.

Look closely at where the shoulder stones stop in the photograph and continue through the product gallery for another view. A front-facing picture does not show every part of the band. If the extent of the accents matters to you, confirm it rather than assuming the stones continue all the way around.

Once you know you enjoy the accented shoulders, compare the metal option you intend to order and its current price. The centre is moissanite in both versions; the smaller stones vary with the metal, as noted above.

## Round Halo Pavé: frame the centre

The Round Halo Pavé adds a visible border of smaller stones around the main stone. In its photograph, you can follow an outer outline as well as the centre’s round edge. The shoulders carry accents too, so the decoration continues beyond the halo.

This is the example I’d explore if you want the setting around the centre to be part of what you notice first. It has more going on at the top of the ring than the plain-band Classic. That can be appealing even if you are not looking for a larger centre stone.

A halo adds an outer frame; it does not increase the centre stone’s diameter. Because the three listings share the same stated centre measurement, this is a useful way to separate centre size from the ring’s decorated outline. The listing does not give us a verified overall halo width to turn that difference into a millimetre claim.

Try tracing the border in the photograph with your eye. Do you enjoy seeing a distinct frame, or do you prefer the centre to meet the surrounding space more directly? Neither answer is more sophisticated. You may love a halo in a close-up but prefer the simpler outline when you picture wearing it.

The accent-material distinction noted above applies to this halo design too, so check the description for your chosen metal when comparing it with the Pavé Solitaire.

## Compare the same feature before changing everything else

It is easy to compare one plain-band ring with a much larger, differently shaped halo and decide the setting alone explains the difference. These three examples help you make a more focused comparison because the listed centre diameter and round outline are shared.

Start with the area immediately around the centre. The Classic leaves it unframed; the Pavé Solitaire adds shoulder detail; the Halo surrounds it with a border. Then move outwards and compare the bands. This order lets you notice where you want the decoration before thinking about other options.

Next, compare the view you would actually enjoy wearing. A close-up is useful for seeing small details, but it can make those details feel more dominant than they would at an ordinary viewing distance. Step back from the screen, or compare a wider photograph from each listing. You are choosing a whole ring, not only a magnified centre.

Keep metal colour consistent while deciding on style where possible. Otherwise, you may be responding to a warmer colour in one photograph rather than to the halo or pavé arrangement. Once you have narrowed the design, explore the available metal colours as a separate choice.

Finally, consider another centre size only if that is still what you want. Our [1–5 carat moissanite size guide](/moissanite-guide/moissanite-ring-sizes-1-to-5-carats) explains the measurement references and visual limits. It answers the size question separately, so you do not need to solve size and setting at the same time.

## Small stones mean more details to look after

GIA notes that detailed [pavé and halo settings can need more cleaning and attention to small stone settings](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/guide-to-ring-settings/). That is a construction consideration, not a prediction that a particular ring will lose stones. A plain-band solitaire still has a centre setting to look after.

For these actual rings, the product pages do not provide a measured setting height. I would not rank them as the lowest, most comfortable or least likely to catch from the style name alone. If that matters for your work or daily routine, ask about the exact ring and look at its side profile.

You can make the enquiry practical: “I wear gloves at work; can you show me the side profile and explain what I should consider?” That gives the seller more useful context than asking whether every halo is suitable for everyday wear.

If you plan to add a wedding band, our [wedding-band fit guide](/moissanite-guide/wedding-band-fit-engagement-ring) explains the profile and contact checks; these product photographs do not establish a tested matching pair.

## Which one would I start with?

| Your first priority | A useful starting point | What to compare next |
|---|---|---|
| Seeing the centre clearly against plain metal | Classic Solitaire | Whether the plain shoulders give you enough detail |
| Keeping the centre outline while decorating the band | Pavé Solitaire | The shoulder-stone arrangement and selected accent material |
| Enjoying a decorated border around the centre | Round Halo Pavé | The outer frame and how it looks with the accented shoulders |
| Finding a ring that works with a particular routine | Any shortlisted design | The actual side profile, fit and wearing advice |

These are starting points, not rules about who should wear each style. If you like simple jewellery but keep returning to the halo, that is worth listening to. A plain-band solitaire can still feel right for a significant occasion.

If you are torn between two, describe what each gives you. “I like the clear centre on this one, but the decorated shoulders on that one” is a much better starting point for a conversation than trying to make yourself fit a style label. You may find the Pavé Solitaire bridges the two preferences, or you may realise you prefer one of the other designs outright.
