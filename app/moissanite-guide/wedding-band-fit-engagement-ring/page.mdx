# How to Match a Wedding Band to Your Engagement Ring

<GuideSchema title="How to Match a Wedding Band to Your Engagement Ring" path="/moissanite-guide/wedding-band-fit-engagement-ring" updated="2026-10-22" />

You may already have an engagement ring you love. Now comes the next question: what will feel right beside it?

My advice is to start with the fit. **A straight wedding band needs enough space beside or beneath the centre setting to sit close. If the setting gets in the way, a curved band may sit around it. And if you love a little gap between your rings, that is a choice too.** The rings should feel comfortable together, with no pressure against a stone or its setting.

As you explore our [moissanite ring collection](/collections/moissanite-rings), look beyond the view from above. Turn your attention to the side photographs. That is where you can begin to see how two rings might sit together.

If you are still deciding whether you want one ring or two, my [engagement ring vs wedding ring guide](/moissanite-guide/engagement-ring-vs-wedding-ring) can help you think that through first.

## Flush fit, a gap or a shaped band?

A **flush fit** simply means the two bands sit closely together, with little or no visible gap. You may also see “flush setting” used for a gemstone set into the metal, but that is a different detail.

I like the thought of leaving room for your own taste here. Perhaps you love two straight lines sitting close together. Perhaps a gentle curve feels more like you. A little breathing space between the rings can be lovely as well.

| What you would like | What to check first |
|---|---|
| Two straight bands sitting close | Whether the wedding band clears the engagement ring's setting, including its lower details |
| A band following the centre setting | Whether the curve or recess suits that exact setting's outline and height |
| A straight band with a visible gap | Whether you like the gap on your hand and where the rings meet as they move |
| A wedding band you will often wear alone | Whether you enjoy its outline and feel without the engagement ring beside it |

Once you find a look you love, ask a jeweller to check where the two rings touch. Looking right and wearing well are both worth your attention.

## Start with the engagement ring's side profile

The part beneath the centre stone is easy to overlook. There is the basket holding the stone, sometimes a rail around it, and the shoulders where the band rises towards the centre. These little details can take up the space where you hoped your wedding band would sit.

So even when a stone sits high, check what is underneath it. A rail may still get in the way. With a lower setting, you may need to explore a curve or accept a gap, but the label alone cannot tell you the answer. The height and shape of the wedding band matter just as much.

If you would like to see the difference drawn out, page 5 of [Casting House's jewellery design guide](https://www.castinghouse.com/images/files/Jewelry%20Design%20Guide-WEB.pdf) shows a straight band beneath a raised setting and a shaped band following a setting that extends outwards.

When choosing online, ask for front and side views of your own ring too. And if you are comparing measurements, take a moment to check what each number means. The size of a stone tells you something different from the size of the setting around it.

**Width and depth are worth asking about separately.** Width tells you how much space the band takes up along your finger. Depth tells you how far it stands out from your skin. If the band has stones, ask for the height including their settings. Something that looks delicate from above can still be too tall to tuck beneath your engagement ring.

## Straight, curved, notched and open wedding bands

Before choosing how much sparkle to add, think about the line you want the band to make. These are the main shapes you can ask about; availability will depend on the range you are browsing.

| Band outline | How it approaches the fit | What I would check |
|---|---|---|
| Straight | Keeps an uninterrupted line beside the engagement ring | Clearance around the centre setting and whether any resulting gap appeals |
| Curved or contour | Bends around part of the engagement ring's outline | The depth and width of the curve, its height, and how it looks worn alone |
| Notched | Has a recess intended to accommodate part of a setting | Whether the recess matches the actual setting rather than just its general shape |
| Open | Has a gap in the wedding band itself to leave space around the centre setting | The position and finish of the ends, comfort, and contact as the rings move |

If a straight band stops at the setting, a curve is worth exploring. Just take care with how that curve is shaped. One may be too shallow, another too deep, and the height can still make a difference. Try to see the two rings together before falling in love with the idea of them.

### What about a wedding band for an oval engagement ring?

You do not automatically need a curved band because your centre stone is oval. Look underneath the oval first. Does the basket reach into the space beside the ring, or is there room for a band to sit below it?

From there, compare a straight band and a contour if you can. Let the fit and the look on your hand guide you. The same approach works for round stones, halos and other shapes. If you are choosing the engagement ring as well, our [moissanite ring buying guide](/moissanite-guide/how-to-choose-a-moissanite-ring) goes through shapes, settings and metals.

## Is a gap between the rings a problem?

You do not have to change a ring you love just to make the two bands look joined. If a little gap feels right to you, there is room for that choice.

What matters is understanding why the gap is there. Is the wedding band meeting part of the setting before it can come closer? Is it pressing against a prong or an exposed stone? Have a jeweller look at those points, and never push the rings together to close the gap.

There is no one gap measurement that suits every pair. Rings move as you wear them, so a photograph can only tell you so much. If a band repeatedly rubs against a stone or its setting, catches, or puts pressure on the setting, stop wearing the pair together until it has been checked. For everyday handling and cleaning, you can turn to our [jewellery care guide](/pages/jewellery-care-guide).

### Would a spacer ring help?

A spacer is a third band worn between the other two to leave a little more room. It can be worth asking about when a stone-set band would otherwise touch a vulnerable part of the neighbouring setting. The jeweller will need to check where all three rings meet.

Remember that you will have another band on your finger, too. A spacer may help with separation, but it will not solve every fit problem. If three rings feel like too much, you can wear your rings on different hands or enjoy the wedding band on its own. Just check the size for the finger you choose; it may differ between hands.

## Check the comfort of the whole stack

This is the part I would take slowly. If you can try the rings, wear them together on the finger you have in mind. Rest your hand, bring your fingers together and gently close your hand. Notice how the bands feel between your fingers and underneath, and whether either one catches the other.

Two bands take up more space than one, so the pair may feel different from your engagement ring alone. That does not mean you should automatically go up half a size. Check the fit of the rings together, and use our [ring size guide](/pages/size-guide) if you need help with the sizing details.

Then take off the engagement ring and look at the band on its own. Can you picture reaching for it on an ordinary morning? A curve you love as part of a pair may feel different by itself. It is a small thing to try, but it can make the choice clearer.

## What to send when asking about a pairing

If you are choosing online and feeling unsure, you do not need to know all the jewellery terms. Start with a few photographs and tell us what you are hoping for.

1. **Your engagement ring:** a product link if available, plus clear front and side photographs. Include its metal, size and any known measurements.
2. **The wedding band you are considering:** its link or photographs, chosen metal and size. Ask for missing measurements of the complete setting, not just the stones.
3. **Your preferred look:** a straight band with or without a gap, or a shaped band following the setting. Mention whether you will wear the band alone too.
4. **Your timing:** the date you need it by, leaving room to confirm the fit and current ordering arrangements before committing.

You can use this as a starting message:

> I love this engagement ring and am looking for a wedding band to wear beside it. I am hoping for [a close fit / a small gap / a curved outline]. Could you help me understand how these two designs would sit together? Have they been tried as a pair, or would you need more photographs or measurements? I would like the band by [date].

[Send us your ring photographs and questions](/pages/contact), and we can start there. We may need more detail or an in-person check before the fit can be confirmed. If you have a shaped band in mind, ask what is possible before making plans around a custom design.

## Choose the pair you want to live with

My hope is that you find a wedding band you love beside your engagement ring, and love just as much on its own if that is how you will wear it. Give yourself time to look at the pair from the side, feel it on your hand and ask the practical questions.

A close-fitting curve and a straight band with a little space can both feel right. Once the fit has been checked, choose the one that feels like yours.
